CAVA PT/Patient Success Story

From fractured bones to back on the court.

How a 20-year-old collegiate basketball player went from a stiff, guarded wrist to supervised court drills by week 12 after a bilateral forearm fracture, with full return to play to follow.

01The Injury

Jackson was competing in a pickup basketball game when he attempted a dunk and got his right arm caught in the rim. The resulting fall put his full body weight through a hyperextended forearm, resulting in a complete structural fracture through both the radius and ulna at the proximal forearm.

At GNMH's Emergency Department, anteroposterior and lateral X-rays confirmed a dislocated proximal forearm fracture with complete breaks in both bones. Physical exam found swelling on the dorsal hand and fingers, though sensation, circulation, and active finger extension remained fully intact — confirming no major nerve or vascular damage.

02The Surgery

Eight weeks before his first PT visit, Jackson underwent Open Reduction and Internal Fixation (ORIF). Both fractured bones were moved back into alignment and secured with metal plates and screws. A cock-up plaster splint was applied after surgery and stayed on through the whole immobilization period (weeks 1 to 8) to protect the hardware and hold the wrist in position. During those weeks he could only move his fingers and shoulder gently.

03What Jackson Told Us

At his first CAVA PT visit, Jackson named four goals:

  • 1Return to basketball: dunk and play at full competition level once his surgeon clears him.
  • 2Daily hand function: writing, typing for class, and driving his car.
  • 3ROM goals: wrist flexion from 50 to 60 degrees and radial deviation from 15 to 20 degrees, then 75 to 80 degrees of flexion by month 6.
  • 4Confidence: overcome anxiety about re-injury at the rim.

SMART goals

  • Flexion: increase right wrist flexion from 50 degrees to 60 degrees within 4 weeks, measured with a goniometer at every visit.
  • Radial deviation: increase right wrist radial deviation from 15 degrees to 20 degrees within 2 weeks, measured with a goniometer at every visit.
  • Long term: reach 75 to 80 degrees of wrist flexion by the end of strengthening (month 6) and return to basketball once the surgeon clears him.
  • Return to play: surgeon clearance plus at least 90 percent grip-strength symmetry on dynamometry.

"Within 2 weeks" and "within 4 weeks" count from the first PT visit at week 8, so these goals fall at week 10 and week 12.

These goals drove every decision in the care plan — from exercise selection to the timeline for progressive loading to the decision to include sports psychology support.

Anatomy of the Injury

Bones, muscles & tissues affected

Understanding which structures were damaged is the foundation of the rehabilitation plan. Each targeted muscle and bone in Jackson's protocol was chosen based on its role in the injury and recovery.

Skeletal Structures

  • R
    Radius — larger forearm bone on the thumb (lateral) side. Bears ~80% of gripping load. Both complete fractures at the proximal end were fixed with ORIF plating.
  • U
    Ulna — medial forearm bone on the pinky side. Forms the elbow hinge. Proximal ulna fracture required fixation to stabilize the forearm's axis of rotation.
  • IO
    Interosseous Membrane — fibrous sheet connecting radius and ulna. Transmits force between both bones and was stressed during the fracture event.

Rehabilitation Target Muscles

  • FCU
    Flexor Carpi Ulnaris — medial forearm; flexes and ulnar deviates the wrist. Primary target for ROM restoration.
  • BR
    Brachialis — deep elbow flexor beneath the biceps. Weakened during immobilization. Primary mover for elbow flexion.
  • TB
    Triceps Brachii (Medial Head) — deepest elbow extensor, active throughout full extension range. Critical for shooting mechanics.
  • DEL
    Deltoid — multi-head shoulder muscle. Maintains overhead reach for shooting and dunking. Anterior head = forward raise; lateral head = abduction.

Neurovascular Status

  • RN
    Radial Nerve — controls wrist and finger extension. Active finger extension was intact at admission — confirming no radial nerve damage.
  • UN
    Ulnar Nerve — controls ring and little finger fine movements. Sensibility confirmed intact, ruling out ulnar nerve injury.
  • VS
    Radial Artery — main arterial supply to the hand. Vascularization confirmed intact at the ER, indicating no vascular compromise.

Connective Tissue & Healing

  • PE
    Periosteum — dense fibrous tissue covering bone. Contains osteoblasts (bone-building cells) critical to fracture union. ORIF hardware protects periosteal blood flow.
  • SK
    Skin & Incision Site — histological biopsy of a removed skin tag confirmed no malignancy. Surgical incisions require protection from stress during early PT.
  • CP
    Cock-up Splint — post-op immobilization device protecting the ORIF hardware while early bone healing begins. Worn through the whole immobilization period (weeks 1 to 8), until PT began.
