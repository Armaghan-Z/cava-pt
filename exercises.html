Anatomy & Physiology

Forearm musculature — Maniken model

Understanding the injured structures helps Jackson actively participate in his recovery. The four key muscles below directly govern forearm rotation and wrist stability.

PT
Forearm Pronation

Pronator Teres

Crosses the elbow obliquely from the medial epicondyle to the lateral radius. Rotates the forearm so the palm faces down. Primary target in early ROM exercises.

SUP
Forearm Supination

Supinator

Wraps around the proximal radius. Returns the palm to face upward. Works antagonistically with pronator teres; weakness here limits overhead ball-handling.

FCR
Wrist Flexion & Abduction

Flexor Carpi Radialis

Runs from the medial epicondyle to the 2nd metacarpal. Flexes and radially deviates the wrist. Essential for grip and ball-handling control in basketball.

ECU
Wrist Extension & Adduction

Extensor Carpi Ulnaris

Extends along the posterior forearm to the 5th metacarpal. Stabilizes the wrist during push-off and passing motions. Requires progressive loading during phases 2–3.

Surgical Hardware

Bilateral ORIF — radial & ulnar fixation

Jackson sustained fractures to both the radius and ulna bilaterally, requiring Open Reduction and Internal Fixation (ORIF) surgery. Titanium plates and screws were inserted to stabilize the fracture sites while healing progresses.

The hardware remains in place long-term and provides a stable scaffold for progressive loading during rehabilitation. Exercise selection must avoid excessive torque forces at the fixation sites, particularly during the first 4 weeks.

  • Radius: lateral forearm bone, primary load-bearer
  • Ulna: medial forearm bone, stabilizes rotation
  • DRUJ: distal radioulnar joint — governs pro/supination
  • Interosseous membrane: connects both bones, transfers forces
The Protocol

Three core therapeutic exercises

Each exercise is chosen for its specificity to the injured structures and its progressive loading profile across the 12-week timeline.

01
Phase 1 — Weeks 1–3 · Acute Protection

Passive Forearm Pro/Supination

Gravity-assisted, pain-free range-of-motion restoration

Starting position

Patient seated with elbow at 90° flexion, supported on a flat table. The opposite hand gently cradles the injured forearm just distal to the elbow joint, providing support without compressing the fracture sites.

Movement

Using gravity and gentle passive assist from the contralateral hand, the forearm is slowly rotated from full supination (palm up) toward pronation (palm down) and back. The wrist should remain in a neutral position throughout — no forced end-range loading. Hold each end position for 3 seconds, breathing steadily.

Precaution

Stop immediately if pain exceeds 3/10 on the pain scale. This exercise must stay in the pain-free arc of motion. Swelling, warmth, or increased pain after exercise indicates excess dosage — reduce reps and notify the PT at the next session.

Prescription
  • Sets3
  • Reps10–15
  • Hold3 sec
  • Rest60 sec
  • Frequency2× daily
Modifications
  • Pain > 3/10: reduce arc by 10°
  • Increased swelling: ice 10 min after
  • Low pain tolerance: water-assisted in warm bath
02
Phase 2 — Weeks 4–8 · Active Strengthening

Wrist Flexion & Extension with Dumbbell

Progressive resistance training for FCR and ECU activation

Starting position

Seated, forearm resting palm-up on a table with the wrist hanging slightly off the edge. Begin with a 1 lb dumbbell (or no weight if weight-bearing is still restricted). Perform flexion sets, then flip the forearm to perform extension sets separately — do not combine into one continuous movement.

Flexion phase

Curl the wrist upward through the full available range (starting near the 50° baseline and progressing toward 80° as swelling resolves). Squeeze briefly at the top, then lower with control over 3 seconds. Tempo: 1 sec up / 1 sec hold / 3 sec down. The eccentric lowering phase is essential for tendon remodeling.

Extension phase

Flip forearm to palm-down. Extend the wrist upward, keeping the fingers relaxed. Focus on moving through the available range without compensating with elbow or shoulder motion. Increase load by 0.5 lb only when 3 sets of 15 reps can be completed with no pain and good form for two consecutive sessions.

Prescription
  • Sets3
  • Reps12–15
  • Tempo1-1-3
  • Load1–3 lbs
  • Frequency3× weekly
Modifications
  • Weak grip: use Theraputty instead of dumbbell
  • Pain at end range: reduce arc by 15°
  • Advanced: add radial/ulnar deviation sets
03
Phase 3 — Weeks 9–12 · Functional Return

Resistance Band Diagonal Chop — Sport-Specific Pattern

Closed-chain neuromuscular re-training for basketball mechanics

Setup

Anchor a light resistance band at shoulder height. Stand perpendicular to the anchor, feet shoulder-width apart in an athletic stance. Grip the band with both hands, injured limb on the distal end. Begin with the arms reaching across the body toward the anchor (loaded position).

Movement

Drive the arms diagonally downward and across the body in a "chopping" pattern, fully extending at the end range. The movement integrates forearm pro/supination, wrist stabilization, shoulder rotation, and core engagement simultaneously — mirroring the mechanics of a basketball chest pass or crossover dribble.

Progression

Weeks 9–10 use a light band with controlled tempo. Weeks 11–12 progress to a medium band with explosive concentric and controlled eccentric phases. The final two sessions may include a medicine ball variant (2 kg) with the team's athletic trainer supervising return-to-sport criteria. Full clearance requires 90% bilateral grip strength symmetry on dynamometry testing.

Prescription
  • Sets4
  • Reps10 each side
  • BandLight → Med
  • Rest90 sec
  • Frequency3× weekly
Modifications
  • Low strength: seated version with lighter band
  • Advanced: add deceleration stop at end range
  • Sport return: medicine ball chest-pass variant

Anatomical Language

Directional terms & reference

Standard anatomical directional terms used throughout this program, referenced from the anatomical position (standing upright, palms forward).

Anterior01

Toward the front of the body. The flexor compartment of the forearm is on the anterior side.

Posterior02

Toward the back of the body. Extensor muscles run along the posterior forearm.

Medial03

Toward the midline. The ulna is the medial bone of the forearm.

Lateral04

Away from the midline. The radius is the lateral bone of the forearm.

Proximal05

Closer to the origin (torso). The elbow joint is proximal to the wrist.

Distal06

Farther from the origin. The wrist is distal to the elbow in the forearm.

Supination07

Forearm rotation so the palm faces upward (superiorly); "holding a bowl of soup."

Pronation08

Forearm rotation so the palm faces downward (inferiorly); "pouring out the soup."

Dorsal09

The back surface of the hand or wrist. Extensor tendons run along the dorsal surface.

Volar / Palmar10

The front surface of the hand. Flexor tendons and the carpal tunnel are volar structures.

Radial Deviation11

Wrist movement toward the thumb side (radius). Limited by the radiocarpal ligaments.

Ulnar Deviation12

Wrist movement toward the pinky side (ulna). Important for writing and racket sports.
